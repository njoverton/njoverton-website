In which Fielder dismisses the myth of the objective observer and crams himself into the lives of Holmes and co. by invitation. This isn’t as radical nor invasive as it seems: weirdly enough I reckon most celebrities would beg for this treatment. By concentrating media reporting of this spectacle into a three-hour bombshell, Elizabeth Holmes resists having her story defined by shaky phone footage and silicon valley gossip. Her story is now defined by what she, herself, has said. A rare luxury in 21st century reporting.

Fielder’s talents lie in a strange, murky realm. His direct, disconcerting questions peel away the facade of truth we operate on to unmask the semi-lingual world of half-thoughts, doubts, and carnal desires we each inhibit. Through intense questioning, parabolic microphones and facial close-ups stretched across meters of silver screen he drowns the audience with information. Blinks become tells and filler words become a sign of guilty hesitation. Figuring out what’s going on is a herculean task, and you’d be forgiven for disagreeing with the person next to you. The complexity of the story grows with the density of information presented.

But we're reminded that half of storytelling is what you leave out. When you show everything you can’t craft a cohesive narrative. Any story you tell will just be at odds with itself. The Faustian bargain Holmes has made, intentionally or not, is that she has traded the privacy of herself, her fiancé, and her children for the absence of any consistent narrative about Theranos. The funny thing about the movie is that the audience doesn’t really know that much more by the end of it. It’s as far from the procedural unfolding of a criminal trial as possible. What we do get, instead, is an entertaining series of suspicions, doubts, and grudges. Nothing is confirmed and everything is left to interpret. 

So after we’ve been shown everything, what will our verdict be? Holmes has lost one case already - but maybe she doesn’t have to lose in the court of public opinion too. Will people shun her as the con artist the courts found her to be? Or will people sympathise with her? After all, maybe we were too hasty. She never sold a share. It’s not a crime to believe in making the world a better place. Maybe, just maybe, we should give her another chance? Who knows. After all, it’s very complicated.
